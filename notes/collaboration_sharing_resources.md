## Collaboration, Sharing, and Resources

## Topics to Discuss

### Norms of data release
- The Bermuda Principles (1996) required nightly release of human genome data, before publication. Was that the right trade-off then? Is it now?
- What is the appropriate embargo, if any, between data generation and public release? Does it differ for sequencing, imaging, proteomics, clinical data?
- Who owns the right to first publication: the group that generated the data, the group that analyzed it, neither?
- How should consortium agreements be structured to protect trainees' first-author opportunities while still honoring the sharing principle?

### Code and computational reproducibility
- Stodden et al. found that even journals with explicit code-sharing policies see low actual compliance. Why are the policies failing, and what would make them work?
- Should reviewers be expected to run code during peer review? Is that realistic?
- What are the minimum standards for a paper's "materials": raw data, processed data, analysis code, environment, random seeds?
- How should software papers be reviewed, and what is the obligation to maintain the software after publication?

### Credit and authorship in collaborations
- When a collaboration produces a paper, how is authorship decided across groups? Who writes the final manuscript, and who controls revision?
- What is the role of the corresponding author in a multi-institution collaboration?
- How should secondary-use papers (data you did not generate) treat the originating group? What counts as adequate acknowledgement?
- Are co-first and co-corresponding authorships honest solutions or ways of papering over disputes?

### Open science and incentives
- McKiernan et al. argue that openness helps individual researchers, not just the community. Do the incentive structures at UW and NIH reward open practice or punish it?
- What are the realistic costs of open data and open code in terms of time, scooping risk, and misuse?
- How should early-career researchers balance openness with the need to build a visible, first-authored portfolio?

### Resources and fairness
- Is it ethical for a well-resourced lab to compete with a lesser-resourced one on the same biological question? What obligations, if any, come with infrastructure?
- Core facilities, compute clusters, and model organisms are shared resources; how should access be prioritized, and by whom?
- When a collaboration fails, who keeps the reagents, the data, the code, the preprint?

## Case Scenarios

### Scenario 1: The consortium paper
You are in a 30-investigator consortium producing a large-scale dataset. Partway through, one site pulls ahead and publishes a preprint using the full dataset to answer a high-profile question, listing only their local group as authors. The data-use agreement is ambiguous on this case. Other sites are furious.

- Was the preprint a violation of the agreement, of the spirit of the agreement, or neither?
- What are the consortium's realistic options now (correction, retraction, formal complaint, amended DUA)?
- How should future consortia structure "permission to publish" decisions?
- What is your role if your PI is on both the publishing site and the consortium steering committee?

### Scenario 2: The collaborator who will not share code
A senior group publishes a high-impact paper using a proprietary analysis pipeline. You try to apply the method to your data and find the published description is not enough to reproduce the result. You email the corresponding author; they respond that the code is "not yet ready for release." Six months later it is still not released.

- What are your options: keep asking, publish a reimplementation, write a comment, move on?
- Is the corresponding author in violation of the journal's policy? Of any professional norm?
- Is it different if the lab is a commercial entity, a competitor, or a close collaborator?
- What should your PI do if your career depends on getting the method to work?

### Scenario 3: The scoop
Your lab and a competing lab are independently working on the same problem. You learn from a conference talk that the other lab is close to submission. Your work is stronger but not yet written up.

- Do you accelerate, submit a preprint, propose a joint submission, or stay the course?
- Is there an ethical difference between "getting ahead" and "slowing them down"?
- What is the role of the PI community, including senior scientists outside both labs, in resolving priority disputes?
- How should the journal respond if both manuscripts arrive within a week of each other?

### Scenario 4: The shared reagent
A postdoc leaves your lab for a faculty position and takes with her cell lines, plasmids, and an analysis pipeline she developed. Two years later she publishes using these resources without acknowledging the origin lab. A reviewer of her next paper asks for the cell lines; she sends them, but your lab now receives frequent requests via her.

- What does the former postdoc owe the origin lab, and what does the origin lab owe her?
- Should material transfer agreements have anticipated this?
- Is the answer different if her faculty appointment was at a less-resourced institution, or a different country?
- How should the two labs communicate going forward so the pattern does not escalate?
