# Ethics, GS 580
## Integrity of Data, Management and Analysis, Scientific Fraud

## Readings

### High-profile cases and investigations
- An Unwelcome Discovery (New York Times, on the Sudbo case)
- Whistle Blowing (Nature, 2021)

### Image and data manipulation
- Bik, Image Manipulation in Biomedical Research
- Research Integrity in the Era of Artificial Intelligence

### Reproducibility and research practice
- Baker, 1,500 scientists lift the lid on reproducibility (Nature, 2016)
- Begley and Ellis, Raise Standards for Preclinical Research (Nature, 2012)

## Topics to Discuss

### Data handling and management
- What does "raw data" mean in your field, and who owns it? The student? The PI? The institution? The funder?
- How long should raw data be retained, and in what form? What happens when a student leaves the lab?
- What is the difference between data cleaning, data selection, and data manipulation? Where are the lines, and who draws them?
- Are electronic lab notebooks a solution, a liability, or both?
- What policies protect against accidental loss of integrity (overwritten files, unversioned code, lost passwords)?

### Recognizing problems
- What are the warning signs that data in a paper or a talk may not be trustworthy?
- What do you do if you suspect a labmate or collaborator is manipulating data? What if you suspect your advisor?
- What institutional mechanisms exist at UW for reporting suspected misconduct, and what protections exist for whistleblowers?
- The Bik paper showed that ~4% of published biomedical papers contain image problems. How do you reconcile that prevalence with the assumption that the literature is broadly trustworthy?

### Fraud vs. error vs. sloppiness
- The federal definition of research misconduct covers fabrication, falsification, and plagiarism (FFP). What does it exclude, and is that the right line?
- What is the difference, in consequence and in culpability, between an honest error, sloppy practice, and fabrication?
- How should corrections, expressions of concern, and retractions differ?
- Who is accountable when junior authors commit fraud on a paper with a senior corresponding author?

### Reproducibility
- Baker's survey found more than 70% of researchers have failed to reproduce another scientist's experiments. Is that a crisis, or is it how science is supposed to work?
- What fraction of irreproducibility is fraud, what fraction is error, and what fraction is normal biological or methodological variability?
- What changes in incentives, publication, or training would most improve reproducibility? What has actually been tried since Begley and Ellis (2012)?

### AI and data integrity
- AI-generated images and text introduce new modes of fabrication. What detection tools exist, and what are their limits?
- Should journals require deposition of raw, un-processed images and of analysis code? Should reviewers be expected to inspect them?

## Case Scenarios

### Scenario 1: The inconvenient replicate
A graduate student runs an experiment in triplicate. Two replicates show the expected effect; one replicate shows the opposite. The student omits the outlier, reasoning that it was probably a technical failure, and reports the mean and SD of the remaining two. The figure makes it into a manuscript submission. A reviewer asks for more replicates. When the student repeats the experiment, the result looks more like the omitted outlier.

- Was the original omission misconduct, bad practice, or acceptable?
- What should the student do now? What should the advisor do?
- What if the paper had already been published before the new replicates came in?
- How should the lab handle outlier policy going forward?

### Scenario 2: The suspicious western blot
You are reviewing a manuscript for a society journal. On close inspection, two loading-control bands in different figures appear to be identical, just rotated. The corresponding author is a senior scientist with no prior flagged record. The other reviewers did not notice.

- What do you put in your review, and what do you communicate only to the editor?
- Do you contact the corresponding author directly?
- If the journal accepts the paper anyway, what is your next step?
- How should the editor handle this if the authors claim a figure-assembly error?

### Scenario 3: The whistle with a cost
A postdoc in a neighboring lab tells you, in confidence, that she has seen her PI instruct a student to "clean up" flow cytometry gates in a way that changes the conclusion of a Nature paper under revision. She is on a J-1 visa, and her PI is writing her recommendation letters. She wants your advice.

- What are her options at UW, and what are the realistic costs of each?
- What are your obligations, having now been told? Are you a bystander, a witness, or neither?
- If the paper is published, is there a point at which the community's interest in correction outweighs her career risk?

### Scenario 4: The AI-generated microscopy image
A reviewer using a generative-AI detection tool flags that a confocal image in a manuscript under review appears to be synthetic. The authors insist the image is real and offer the raw microscope file. The raw file validates, but two other images in the same paper fail the same detector.

- What does the reviewer do with the mixed result? Is an AI detector evidence of misconduct?
- What standards should journals set for "raw data" submission, and what is realistic?
- How does the burden of proof shift when detection tools are imperfect in both directions?
