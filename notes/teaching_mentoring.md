# Ethics, GS 580
## Teaching and Mentoring

## Readings

### What good mentorship looks like
- Davis et al., 2023. How to be a great mentor
- Weiss, 2023. What I owe my mentor
- The Care and Maintenance of your Advisor

### Power, climate, and structure
- Maestre, 10 Simple Rules for Building an Antiracist Lab
- Barres, Stop Blocking Postdocs' Paths to Success (Nature, 2017)
- Evans et al., Evidence for a mental health crisis in graduate education

## Topics to Discuss

### Defining the relationship
- What do a student and advisor actually owe each other? Are those obligations symmetric?
- What is the difference between a mentor, an advisor, a boss, and a sponsor? Should one person be all of these?
- How should mentoring expectations be made explicit at the start of a graduate career? What does a useful mentoring compact look like?
- What is the appropriate role of committee members, and how should a student use them when the primary advisor relationship is strained?

### Power dynamics
- Graduate students and postdocs depend on their advisor for funding, letters, authorship, and visa status. How does that dependence shape what can be said openly?
- What changes when the advisor is junior, unfunded, or under tenure pressure?
- What forms of behavior from an advisor cross the line from "demanding" into "abusive," and who gets to draw that line?
- What structural mechanisms at UW exist for students whose primary advising relationship breaks down, and how well do they work?

### Climate and equity
- The antiracist-lab piece argues that inclusion is an active practice, not a passive stance. What does that look like week to week in a lab?
- Who is responsible for the climate of a lab: the PI, the senior trainees, everyone, the institution?
- How should a lab respond when a member behaves in a way that harms others? Does the answer depend on who the offender is?
- Barres argues that postdoc careers are being blocked by structural factors, not individual shortcomings. Which of those factors apply here, and which are in the lab's control?

### Mental health
- The Evans et al. paper found rates of anxiety and depression in graduate students roughly six times the general population. What features of graduate training are causing this, and which are fixable?
- What should a PI do when they suspect a trainee is in crisis? Where are the lines between "I care about you" and "I am not qualified to handle this"?
- What should a peer do? A committee member?
- How do productivity norms (papers, grants, hours) have to change, if at all, to make graduate training sustainable?

## Case Scenarios

### Scenario 1: The disappearing advisor
A third-year graduate student's advisor has not held a one-on-one meeting with them in six months. The advisor is on multiple grant review panels and is frequently traveling. When the student emails with questions, replies come in weeks, or not at all. The student's committee is not aware of the gap. The student is competent and productive but is starting to drift scientifically.

- What is the student's obligation to communicate the problem, and to whom?
- What are the committee's obligations? Should the chair reach out to the advisor?
- If the advisor is supportive when present but absent most of the time, is that mentorship?
- What if the advisor is a well-funded star whose reputation is central to the student's career prospects?

### Scenario 2: The reference letter with teeth
A postdoc finishing in the lab has been a solid but not spectacular performer. The PI sits down to write a letter for a faculty search. The postdoc is applying to departments where the PI has close colleagues on the hiring committee. A brutally honest letter may end the candidacy; a glowing letter misrepresents the work.

- What does the PI owe the postdoc? The hiring committee? The field?
- Is there a responsibility to give the postdoc the letter's content before it is sent?
- Is "damning with faint praise" an ethical compromise or a cowardly one?
- How should the postdoc have chosen their referees, and what should they have asked beforehand?

### Scenario 3: The crying student
A graduate student comes into the PI's office in tears, says they have been sleeping two hours a night for a week, and describes thoughts that worry the PI. The student asks the PI not to tell anyone. The PI is not a clinician.

- What does the PI do in the next hour? The next day?
- Is a promise of confidentiality binding when safety is at stake?
- What does the PI owe to the rest of the lab, who will notice the student's absence?
- How should labs prepare for this scenario before it happens?

### Scenario 4: The senior trainee who punches down
A postdoc in the lab is scientifically brilliant and a significant driver of the group's output. Two junior graduate students independently report that the postdoc is dismissive, takes credit in meetings, and has made remarks they experienced as demeaning. The postdoc denies all of it and points to their productivity.

- What does the PI owe the junior students? The postdoc? The other lab members who are watching?
- Is "productivity" ever a defense? Should it shift consequences?
- What are the realistic options, from documented conversation to lab transfer to termination, and what are the costs of each?
