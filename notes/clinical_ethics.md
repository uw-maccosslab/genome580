## Topics to Discuss

### Privacy and re-identification
- When is genetic data truly anonymous? The 2013 Gymrek et al. work showed that surname inference from Y-chromosome data and public genealogy databases is feasible. What does "de-identified" mean in 2026?
- Is informed consent in 2010 still valid for a 2026 re-identification attack that the original study could not have anticipated?
- Who is the appropriate gatekeeper of genotype-phenotype databases: the NIH, the institution, the PI, the participants?
- What obligations do researchers have when a technical advance makes previously released data identifiable?

### Law enforcement access
- The Golden State Killer case used GEDmatch, a consumer genealogy database, to identify a suspect via a distant cousin. Is that a legitimate use of public data?
- What are the differences, ethically, between a warrant to a forensic database, a warrant to a research biobank, and a query to a consumer genealogy site?
- Should research participants be warned that their relatives' genomes may expose them to law enforcement?
- Who enforces the lines, and are the protections symmetric across socioeconomic and racial groups?

### Returning results
- Should researchers return individual genetic results to participants? Always, never, or only for "medically actionable" variants?
- Who pays for the confirmatory clinical testing, the genetic counseling, and the long-term follow-up?
- What does "actionable" even mean when evidence changes over time?
- What do you do when a participant did not want to know, but a relative in the same study does?

### GINA and its limits
- GINA prohibits genetic discrimination in health insurance and employment. What does it not cover (life insurance, long-term-care insurance, disability insurance, the military)? Are those gaps defensible?
- How should a PI describe GINA protections during consent, given that most participants do not read consent forms?
- Does GINA protect against algorithmic inference of genetic information from non-genetic data (e.g., medical records, wearables)?

### Germline editing
- He Jiankui's CCR5-edited twins were born in 2018. What about that project was unethical: the science, the consent, the secrecy, or the act of germline editing itself?
- Where is the line between therapy and enhancement, and who gets to draw it?
- Could a properly conducted germline-editing clinical trial ever be ethical? Under what conditions?
- What is the global research community's responsibility when a researcher operates outside the norms of their field?

## Case Scenarios

### Scenario 1: The re-identifiable dataset
A graduate student is about to deposit a whole-genome sequencing dataset for 2,000 participants in dbGaP. The dataset is de-identified by institutional standards. A colleague points out that when the dataset is combined with a publicly available surname-inference tool and a genealogy database, roughly a quarter of the participants are plausibly re-identifiable.

- Does the deposit go forward as planned?
- What is the IRB's role here, and is the existing consent broad enough?
- Should the participants be re-contacted? What would you tell them?
- Is the answer different if the funder (NIH) requires open data deposit?

### Scenario 2: The incidental finding
A population-scale sequencing study discovers a clearly pathogenic BRCA1 variant in a 29-year-old female participant. The consent form said incidental findings of "significant medical relevance" might be returned. The study was not designed to do clinical-grade variant confirmation, and the lab is not CLIA-certified.

- What does the study return, to whom, and with what caveats?
- Who pays for the confirmatory clinical test?
- What is the obligation to the participant's first-degree relatives, who did not consent to anything?
- What if the participant asked at enrollment not to be told anything?

### Scenario 3: The familial search hit
A state crime lab contacts your institution. They have a crime-scene sample and have run a partial match against a commercial genealogy database. The closest match points to a participant in a long-running UW research study. They ask for the participant's contact information, noting they will get a warrant if necessary.

- What does the institution do before the warrant arrives?
- What did the consent form promise, and does it survive a subpoena?
- Is there an obligation to notify the participant? Their family?
- How should future consent forms be written, given this is now foreseeable?

### Scenario 4: The collaborator abroad
A PI at UW is a coauthor on a paper describing a new gene-editing approach. A collaborator at an overseas institution mentions, in passing, that they are considering a clinical application in embryos if regulatory conditions "work out." The UW PI's contribution is purely technical and predates any clinical plan.

- What is the UW PI's obligation now that they have been told?
- What are the institutional options: withdraw from the paper, report to UW, report to the overseas institution, do nothing?
- Does the answer depend on how credible the clinical plan seems?
- What does the He Jiankui case teach about the costs of assuming someone else will stop an ethically questionable project?
