## Topics to Discuss

### Authorship
- Who should decide when a project is ready to publish? Who decides what content is included?
- How should the order of authors be decided? Can authorship be decided at the start of a project?
- The ICMJE defines authorship through four criteria (substantial contribution, drafting/revising, final approval, accountability). Are these criteria realistic for large team science or computational papers?
- How should contributor statements (CRediT taxonomy) be used? Do they solve or shift the authorship problem?
- What happens when data in a paper does not hold up? How is responsibility distributed, especially across large collaborations? Who decides whether to retract?
- What counts as a conflict of interest, and how should it be reported?

### Peer review
- What is the role of a reviewer: quality control, appropriateness of citations, ethical oversight, or gatekeeping?
- When is it appropriate for a reviewer to ask for additional experiments? When does that become reviewer overreach?
- What information belongs in a written review, and what should be kept to the editor?
- Should a designated reviewer ever delegate the review to a student or postdoc? Under what conditions?
- How does one actually learn to write a manuscript review? Should training be formalized?
- How should a reviewer handle a manuscript that overlaps with their own unpublished work?

### AI in publishing
- Can a large language model be an author? The major journals say no. Why, and does the ICMJE accountability criterion settle it?
- What uses of AI in writing and analysis require disclosure, and where does the line fall between "tool" and "coauthor"?
- How should reviewers handle suspected AI-generated text or figures? What about AI-generated reviews?
- What are journals' obligations to detect AI-generated or AI-assisted submissions, and what are authors' obligations to disclose?

## Case Scenarios

### Scenario 1: The postdoc, the technician, and the pilot data
Ali is a new postdoc in Dr. Young's lab. Ali wrote a risky postdoctoral proposal, funded by a visionary agency, that was based on an idea of Dr. Young's but whose technical feasibility Ali worked out. When Ali arrived, another postdoc, Karen, had already initiated the project because her original project had been reassigned to a student whose committee demanded more work. Karen completed the pilot studies, with substantial help from an experienced technician. Karen drafts a paper listing only herself and Young as authors. Ali argues he contributed the intellectual framework; the technician argues the project could not have progressed without her; Karen argues she alone directed and executed the work.

- Who should be authors on the manuscript?
- How much technical contribution is needed to merit authorship? Is a single figure enough?
- How much understanding of the science should an author be expected to have?
- How should Dr. Young have handled the original project reassignment? Should he have asked Ali before redirecting Karen onto Ali's project?
- How could this authorship dispute have been avoided at the outset?

### Scenario 2: One big paper or several smaller ones
Paula, a new assistant professor, and two graduate students have been working on a series of related experiments for several years. They face a choice: publish a single comprehensive paper in a high-visibility journal with one first author, or split the work into shorter papers so each student can be first author. Paula favors the single paper; her students argue the single paper would hurt their career prospects because neither could point to a first-authored publication.

- Are the students justified in asking for separate papers if the experiments are genuinely a series?
- If they go with one paper, how should the author order be handled? Are co-first authorships an honest solution or an inflation?
- If they publish a single paper, how should they communicate each student's role to fellowship and job review committees?

### Scenario 3: The ChatGPT-drafted methods section
A graduate student uses a large language model to draft the introduction and methods section of a paper, then revises the text heavily. The PI is a coauthor and did not know the draft started in an LLM. A reviewer flags suspicious phrasing and asks the authors whether AI was used.

- Does the student need to disclose the use of the LLM? To the PI? To the journal?
- Is the answer different if the LLM was used to polish grammar versus to generate content?
- What is the PI's responsibility for understanding how text in their manuscripts was produced?
- If journal policy required disclosure and the student did not disclose, what are the consequences, and who is accountable?
